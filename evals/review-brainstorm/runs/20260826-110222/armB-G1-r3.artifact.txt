# Blind angle map — Palisade incident notification delivery

Produced from the topic statement alone, before record contact:
"Palisade (incident-management SaaS) — how we deliver incident notifications to
on-call responders. Delivery mechanism, escalation policy, what we owe a customer
when a page fails to land."

No repo, no prior session artifacts consulted for this map.

## Delivery mechanism
- Channel set: push vs SMS vs voice call vs email vs chat-ops (Slack/Teams). Industry
  baseline for critical paging treats **phone calls** as the most reliable wake channel —
  push/SMS are both easy to sleep through (silent mode, Do Not Disturb, dead battery,
  no data signal, notification permission revoked, app force-quit/background-killed).
- iOS-specific: bypassing silent mode / Focus / Do Not Disturb requires Apple's
  **Critical Alerts entitlement**, a distinct, selectively-granted capability — not the
  same problem as "waking a backgrounded app."
- Whether push delivery is federated through a single vendor (single point of failure
  if that vendor has an outage) vs multi-provider redundancy.
- Total-connectivity-loss case: if the phone has no signal at all, no channel lands —
  does the policy assume this can't happen, or account for it?
- Payload sensitivity / lock-screen exposure of incident data.
- Delivery observability: receipts, webhooks, audit trail.
- Notification-storm handling during mass-incident (rate limiting, dedup).
- Cost model: push (near-free) vs SMS (per-segment) vs voice (per-minute) at scale.

## Escalation policy
- Timing: how long unacknowledged before next hop; whether this is uniform or
  configurable per rotation/severity.
- Chain depth and terminal behavior: what happens if every hop, including the final
  one, fails to acknowledge — is there a floor, or does escalation just stop?
- Interaction between multiple channels *within* one hop (e.g. push then SMS fallback
  for the same responder) and escalation to the *next* responder — do these timers
  race, and which wins?
- Claim/ack race conditions across simultaneous responders.
- Schedule/rotation edge cases: shift handoff mid-page, overrides, holidays.
- Manual override / snooze.

## What we owe the customer when a page fails to land
- Definition of "failed to land": undelivered vs delivered-but-unacknowledged vs
  acknowledged-but-late — these are different failure modes with different owed
  responses.
- Real-time customer-facing signal (do they find out during the incident, or only via
  a support ticket after the fact?).
- SLA / credit commitments tied to plan tier.
- Liability boundary: Palisade's infra fault vs. carrier/push-vendor outage.
- Retention window for evidence backing "I never got paged" disputes, and whether it
  matches any stated support/SLA commitment window.
- Postmortem or root-cause obligation.

**1. [Critical] The goal line's third pillar — "what we owe a customer when a page
fails to land" — has zero decisions and zero open threads.**
Decisions touched: none directly; this is an absence.
The record covers delivery mechanism (D1, D2, D4, D5) and escalation (D6, D7) and
internal observability (D3, D8), but nothing customer-facing: no definition of what

ruling almost certainly changes the record here, at minimum by adding an open thread.

**2. [Critical] D2's SMS fallback (5 min) and D6's escalation (3 min) race, and D6 wins
first — undermining D2's own rationale.**
Decisions touched: D2, D6.
D2's stated purpose is to reach a responder whose push failed silently (phone off,
app force-quit) via SMS, "without paying for SMS on the happy path." But D6 escalates
the page to the *next* responder at 3 minutes — two minutes before D2's SMS fallback
would ever fire for the original responder. So in exactly the phone-off scenario D2
exists to cover, the incident has already moved to someone else by the time the SMS
would go out. Neither decision says whether the original responder's SMS still fires
after they've been superseded, whether escalation channel-hops restart their own
3-min/5-min clocks per hop, or whether there's a single global timer. As written, the
two mechanisms don't compose — a ruling either re-times one of them or defines how they
interact.
Resolution: pick one — SMS fallback fires before the first escalation hop (i.e. push
timeout < 3 min), or the two are declared to run in parallel with escalation-supersedes
semantics made explicit.

**3. [Critical] D4's rationale rests on two vendor claims I believe are inaccurate,
under a workspace with no fact-checker map to confirm against — flagging as
unverifiable/likely-wrong rather than settled.**
Decision touched: D4 (and by extension D2, which calls the 5-min fallback "generous"
against D4's guarantee).
- "FCM ... its high-priority messages wake a backgrounded iOS app without us holding a
  separate APNs entitlement" — to my knowledge FCM does not remove the APNs
  dependency on iOS; you still register an APNs auth key with Firebase, and FCM routes
  iOS delivery through APNs under the hood. Separately, and not addressed at all: making
  a page audible through silent mode / Focus / Do Not Disturb — the actual failure mode
  that matters for waking a sleeping on-call responder — requires Apple's Critical
  Alerts entitlement, which is a distinct, selectively-granted capability, not a
  byproduct of "backgrounded app wake." The record conflates "app receives the push in
  background" with "phone makes noise despite DND," which are different problems.
- "FCM guarantees delivery inside five seconds" — I'm not aware of any such published
  SLA from FCM; it's a best-effort service. If that guarantee doesn't exist, D2's "the
  five-minute fallback is generous against that guarantee" loses its justification too.
If a "Confident" architecture decision is standing on claims that don't hold up, and I
can't check them against a source in this workspace, that's exactly what should go back
to whoever can verify against Firebase's/Apple's actual docs before D4 stays Confident.
Resolution: verify both claims against current FCM/APNs documentation; if either is
wrong, D4 and D2's "generous" framing need to be re-argued, and DND-bypass behavior
needs its own explicit decision.

**4. [Important] No voice/phone-call channel appears anywhere, and it's never
explicitly rejected — unlike D7, which shows an actual weighed trade-off.**
Decisions touched: D1 (by omission).
Standard practice for critical on-call paging treats phone calls as the most reliable
wake channel precisely because push and SMS are both easy to sleep through. D1 argues
push-over-SMS on cost grounds but never mentions voice as an option considered and
declined — contrast with D7, which explicitly names the counterargument it overrode.
The absence here reads as unconsidered rather than ruled out. This may be legitimate
v1 scope-cutting, but as written there's no record of that judgment having been made.
Resolution: either confirm voice was considered and out of scope (worth one sentence in
D1's rationale or a new open thread), or treat this as a live gap.

**5. [Important] Escalation has no defined terminal behavior — if the incident
commander (final hop) also doesn't acknowledge, nothing in the record says what
happens next.**
Decision touched: D6.
This is a concrete instance of goal-line pillar 3 (finding 1): "a page fails to land"
most sharply means every hop failed, and the record is silent on it precisely where the
customer-owed answer matters most.
Resolution: define the floor — repeat the chain, alert a broader group, or something
else — and state it, even provisionally, as an open thread if not resolved.

**6. [Important] D8 is more machinery than the stated goal requires, and its cadence
undercuts any real-time customer commitment.**
Decision touched: D8 (and D3, which it reconciles against).
The stated goal is "a second source of truth on whether pages actually landed." Cheaper
shape: have D3's status rows populated directly from provider webhooks (which D8 is
already ingesting) with real-time alerting on stuck/non-terminal states, rather than
standing up a second independent state-derivation pipeline that only reconciles once a
night. As designed, D8 can't support any real-time "we owe you an answer during the
incident" commitment (see finding 1) — a divergence found at 2am is reported the
following night. If nightly batch reconciliation is genuinely the intent, that's fine,
but it should be named as a deliberate limitation, not left implicit under "Assumed."
Resolution: either fold D8's webhook ingestion directly into D3 with real-time
alerting, or keep D8 as designed but state explicitly that it's a slow-lane audit, not
part of any customer-facing failure response.
